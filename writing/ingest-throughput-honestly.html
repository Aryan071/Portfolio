What "49K spans per second" actually means

A throughput number without its conditions is a slogan. Here is exactly what my TraceLens ingest figure measures, what limits it, and what it does not say.

Three different numbers

When I load-tested the ingest path I tracked three rates, because they answer different questions:

  • Offered: how fast the load generator sends spans.
  • Accepted: how fast the receiver acknowledges them.
  • Landed: how fast they are really stored in ClickHouse.

A system can accept faster than it stores, until a buffer fills and it starts rejecting. So the honest question is: at what offered rate does it keep accepting without rejecting anything?

The results

SetupResult
1 ingest processCeiling of about 24K spans/s (the process is CPU-bound; most time goes to converting spans to rows in Python)
4 processes, offered 50K spans/s49.1K accepted, p99 latency 223 ms, 0 rejections
4 processes, pushed to the limit (HTTP)About 81K spans/s, roughly 3.4x the single process

So "about 49K spans per second with no rejections" is the accepted rate when 50K is offered to four processes. The maximum I measured was higher (about 81K), and a single process tops out near 24K.

What I took from it

  1. Find the real bottleneck. The ceiling was one Python process pinned on conversion work, not the network or ClickHouse. Adding processes, not tuning the database, moved the number.
  2. Report the condition with the number. "49K" means nothing without "at 50K offered, 4 processes, 0 rejected, p99 223 ms".
  3. Check that nothing is lost. I also tested that every acknowledged span ends up in ClickHouse exactly once, including through shutdowns and an outage.

What this does not show

These runs use synthetic traces on one machine. They show where the ingest path saturates, not how it behaves on production traffic with a different mix of span sizes.
